Pituitary Gland & Growth Hormone (HGH): How It Works and Why It Matters

A clear, science-based guide to the pituitary gland growth hormone system: what the pituitary does, how GH is released and controlled, and what happens when levels are too low or too high.

In short: the pituitary gland, a pea-sized organ at the base of the brain, makes growth hormone (GH, also written HGH for human growth hormone). GH drives childhood growth and keeps body composition and metabolism balanced throughout life.

What Is the Pituitary Gland?

The pituitary gland is often called the "master gland" because it releases hormones that control other endocrine organs, including the thyroid, adrenal glands and gonads. It is about the size of a pea and sits in a small bony hollow at the base of the skull called the sella turcica, directly beneath the hypothalamus, which controls it.

Growth hormone is made by specialised anterior pituitary cells called somatotrophs, which are the most numerous cell type in the gland.

What Is Growth Hormone (GH / HGH)?

Growth hormone, also known as somatotropin, is a protein hormone of 191 amino acids. The pituitary gland growth hormone axis is essential for normal height in children and continues to matter in adults, supporting muscle, bone, fat metabolism and energy.

What GH does in the body

  • Growth: stimulates growth of bones and cartilage at the growth plates during childhood and adolescence.
  • IGF-1 production: prompts the liver to release insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), which mediates many of GH's growth effects.
  • Body composition: promotes protein synthesis and lean tissue while encouraging the breakdown of stored fat (lipolysis).
  • Metabolism: raises blood glucose by opposing insulin's action, which helps protect against low blood sugar during fasting.
  • Bone health: supports bone remodelling and density in adults.

How the Pituitary Controls GH Release

GH is not released steadily. The pituitary secretes it in pulses, mainly under the control of two hypothalamic hormones and one stomach hormone:

  • GHRH (growth hormone-releasing hormone) stimulates GH release.
  • Somatostatin inhibits GH release.
  • Ghrelin, from the stomach, also stimulates GH release.

Rising IGF-1 and GH levels feed back to the hypothalamus and pituitary to switch release down, a classic negative feedback loop.

GH output is highest in puberty and falls gradually with age, a pattern sometimes called the "somatopause."

Pituitary Growth Hormone Disorders

Growth hormone deficiency (GHD)

GHD means the pituitary does not make enough GH. In children it causes slow growth and short stature. In adults it can bring increased body fat (especially around the waist), reduced muscle mass and strength, low energy, and poorer bone density and lipid profile.

Common causes: congenital pituitary conditions, pituitary or hypothalamic tumours and their surgery or radiation treatment, traumatic brain injury, and infiltrative disease. Sometimes no cause is found.

Growth hormone excess

Too much GH, nearly always due to a benign pituitary adenoma, causes gigantism when it occurs in childhood before growth plates close, and acromegaly in adults. Acromegaly leads to enlarged hands, feet and facial features, joint pain, sleep apnoea, and higher risk of diabetes and heart disease.

How GH Problems Are Diagnosed

Because GH is released in pulses, a single random blood test is rarely useful. Specialists generally use:

  1. IGF-1 blood level, which is stable across the day and reflects overall GH activity.
  2. Stimulation tests (such as insulin tolerance, glucagon or macimorelin tests) to check for deficiency.
  3. Suppression test (oral glucose tolerance test) to confirm excess in suspected acromegaly.
  4. Pituitary MRI to look for tumours or structural problems.
  5. Assessment of the other pituitary hormones, since several may be affected together.

Treatment and Medical Supervision

Confirmed GH deficiency is treated with recombinant human growth hormone given by regular injection, with the dose tailored and monitored by an endocrinologist using IGF-1 levels and clinical response. Excess GH is usually treated with surgery on the pituitary adenoma, medication (such as somatostatin analogues), and sometimes radiotherapy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the pituitary gland do with growth hormone?

The anterior pituitary makes and releases growth hormone in pulses. GH acts directly on tissues and signals the liver to make IGF-1, which drives growth and metabolism.

When is GH release highest?

Most GH is released during deep sleep, with extra pulses after intense exercise and during fasting. Release peaks in puberty and declines with age.

What causes growth hormone deficiency?

Congenital pituitary problems, pituitary tumours or their treatment, head injury, radiation and other hypothalamic or pituitary disease. In some cases no cause is identified.

What happens if the pituitary makes too much GH?

In children it causes gigantism; in adults, acromegaly. It is usually due to a benign pituitary adenoma and is treatable.

Is HGH safe to use without a doctor?

No. HGH should only be used for a diagnosed condition under specialist supervision. Misuse carries real health risks.
